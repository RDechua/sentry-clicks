## 0. Document Purpose and Reader Guidance

This document specifies the design and success criteria for **Sentry**, a mobile ad-click fraud detection system built as a portfolio project demonstrating Trust & Safety engineering competency. It is written before implementation begins, in the style of a real industry PRD, and is itself a project deliverable.

A reader should leave this document able to answer:

1. What harm is this system designed to reduce, to whom, and why does it matter?
2. What does the system do, what does it explicitly *not* do, and how is "success" measured?
3. How would a sophisticated adversary attempt to evade it, and how does the design account for that?
4. What does the enforcement workflow look like end-to-end, including human review and appeals?
5. What are the known limitations, risks, and open questions, and how would they be addressed at production scale?

Sections marked **[OUT OF SCOPE]** describe deliberate exclusions; sections marked **[OPEN QUESTION]** are unresolved decisions surfaced for visibility rather than hidden.

## 1. Problem Statement and Harm Model

### 1.1 The Domain

Mobile advertising operates on a pay-per-click or pay-per-install basis: advertisers pay ad networks (Google Ads, Meta, AppLovin, etc.) each time a user clicks an ad or installs the advertised app. The integrity of this system depends on clicks and installs being attributable to *genuine human intent*. When clicks are generated by automated systems, paid click farms, or compromised devices, the economic model breaks down.

### 1.2 Harm Model

Click fraud causes four categories of harm, in approximate order of dollar magnitude:

**H1. Advertiser financial loss.** Advertisers pay for clicks that have zero conversion probability. Industry estimates of click fraud loss vary widely, but credible figures place global ad fraud losses in the tens of billions of US dollars annually. Small and mid-sized advertisers, who lack sophisticated internal fraud teams, bear disproportionate damage.

**H2. Ecosystem trust erosion.** Advertisers who experience unaddressed fraud reduce ad spend on the affected network, reduce trust in network-reported metrics, or shift to competing networks with better fraud protection. The harm is not only the immediate dollar loss but the long-term reduction in platform value.

**H3. Honest publisher revenue dilution.** Ad networks share revenue with publishers (app developers showing ads). When fraudulent publishers earn revenue through fake clicks, honest publishers receive a smaller share of the advertiser pool. This is a transfer of revenue from honest to fraudulent actors.

**H4. Distorted optimization signals.** Ad networks use click data to train ranking and targeting models. Fraudulent clicks pollute training data and degrade the relevance of ads shown to real users — a second-order harm whose magnitude is hard to quantify but real.

### 1.3 Affected Stakeholders

| Stakeholder | Harm experienced | Counter-incentive |
|---|---|---|
| Advertisers | Wasted ad spend, distorted attribution | Detection benefits them; they fund networks that detect well |
| Honest publishers | Revenue dilution | Detection benefits them; they tolerate stricter rules |
| Ad networks | Trust loss, regulatory exposure | Detection has cost (compute, false positives); benefit is long-term |
| End users | Slower apps from ad-laden fraud sites; rarely direct financial harm | Indirect beneficiaries of cleaner ecosystem |
| Fraudsters (adversaries) | N/A — they benefit from undetected fraud | Actively adapt to evade detection |

### 1.4 Why Now

The TalkingData dataset reflects a real production fraud problem at a real company (TalkingData, a Chinese mobile data services platform serving ~70% of active Chinese mobile devices). The dataset's authenticity is what makes this project credible. A dataset of ~200M ad clicks over four days, with a ground-truth label of whether each click led to an app install, provides a realistic substrate for the fraud detection methodology this PRD specifies.

### 1.5 Problem Statement (One Sentence)

**Build a system that ingests mobile ad-click data, identifies clicks unlikely to represent genuine human intent, and routes detected cases through a tiered enforcement workflow that balances automated action against human review at economically rational thresholds.**

## 2. Goals, Non-Goals, and Success Metrics

### 2.1 Goals

**G1. Detection capability.** Produce a strongly discriminative model on a held-out test set drawn from the training distribution: **ROC-AUC ≥ 0.95**, with **PR-AUC reported as the primary honest metric** for the ~0.25%-positive class and required to sit far above the random and single-feature baselines. The goal is "competitive with serious public work," not "state of the art."

> **Amendment (2026-06-10, after Task 4.7).** This goal originally read "precision-recall AUC of at least 0.85," with the parenthetical "published Kaggle solutions cluster between 0.86 and 0.98 PR-AUC." That parenthetical was wrong: the Kaggle TalkingData competition was scored on **ROC-AUC**, and the 0.86–0.98 figures are ROC-AUC, not PR-AUC. The 0.85 PR-AUC target was therefore a metric-scale error — 0.85 PR-AUC on a 0.25%-positive problem would be near-superhuman. The delivered model scores test **ROC-AUC 0.972** (competitive with that published range) and test **PR-AUC 0.559** (≈220× the base rate, well above the 0.11 single-feature and 0.026 linear baselines) — a strong, honestly-evaluated result. The goal is corrected to a dual-metric statement rather than carrying a target that was an error. Full reasoning: `decisions.md`, 2026-06-10 (Week 4 retrospective + this amendment).

**G2. Cost-aware threshold tuning.** Demonstrate the ability to tune the detection threshold not against a fixed metric, but against an explicitly modeled cost function combining false-positive cost (blocking a legitimate click) and false-negative cost (allowing a fraudulent click). The chosen threshold must be defensible in dollar terms.

**G3. Triage queue design.** Produce a written and code-realized specification for a three-tier enforcement workflow (auto-block, human review, sample-for-QA) with explicit confidence thresholds and expected case volumes.

**G4. Policy and adversary documentation.** Produce written specifications (`policy.md`, `adversary-model.md`) demonstrating the candidate can think about fraud as a policy problem, not only a classification problem.

**G5. Production-shaped engineering.** Deliver a containerized, tested, modular codebase with clean separation between data, features, models, evaluation, and triage layers. The repository must be runnable end-to-end by a reader with Docker installed and no other setup.

**G6. Methodological rigor.** Every non-obvious decision — feature choices, train/test split strategy, sampling, threshold selection, evaluation metric — must be documented with the alternatives considered and the reason for selection.

### 2.2 Non-Goals

The following are explicitly *not* goals of this project. They are surfaced because a senior reader would otherwise wonder why they are absent.

**N1. State-of-the-art leaderboard performance.** The goal is methodological soundness and production-shaped thinking, not winning the Kaggle competition. Aggressive feature engineering for marginal AUC gains is deliberately deprioritized.

**N2. Deep learning models.** Gradient-boosted decision trees (LightGBM/XGBoost) are the established state of the art for tabular fraud detection and run cheaply. Neural approaches are excluded to keep scope tight.

**N3. Real-time / streaming inference.** Batch inference is the only mode supported. Real-time architecture is discussed in `architecture.md` as future work but not implemented.

**N4. Production deployment.** No actual cloud deployment, API server, or service infrastructure beyond a Dockerized CLI. Deployment patterns are discussed conceptually.

**N5. A working web dashboard.** Visualizations are produced as static plots and HTML reports, not as an interactive application. A dashboard layer is documented as future work.

**N6. Multi-modal data.** No image, text, or behavioral biometrics — only the tabular click data provided by the dataset.

**N7. Adversarial machine learning research.** The adversary model in §4 considers evasion strategies and the system is designed to be robust to documented adversary capabilities, but the project does not perform formal adversarial training or red-team the model with crafted inputs.

## 4. Threat Model and Adversary

This section summarizes the threat model; the full adversary document is `adversary-model.md`. The summary is included here because no T&S PRD is complete without it.

### 4.1 Adversary Capabilities

We model three adversary tiers:

**Tier 1 — Naïve.** Runs unmodified emulators or simple scripts. Generates clicks from a small number of IPs in tight time windows. Easily detected by velocity and IP-reuse features. Represents perhaps 30% of fraud volume.

**Tier 2 — Adapted.** Uses rotating residential proxies, varies User-Agent strings, paces clicks to mimic human inter-click intervals. Operates across many devices. Defeats naive rules; detection requires multivariate behavioral features. Represents the bulk of fraud volume (perhaps 60%).

**Tier 3 — Sophisticated.** Operates real (physical or cloud-emulated) devices with stolen or rented user profiles. Each device generates a low volume of fraud blended with legitimate use. Detection is statistically possible only at aggregate / population level. Represents the long tail (~10%) but disproportionate damage per case.

### 4.2 Key Evasion Strategies

| Strategy | Adversary tier | Detection counter |
|---|---|---|
| IP rotation via residential proxies | 2, 3 | Device-IP graph features; ASN reputation |
| User-agent randomization | 1, 2 | Less informative as a single feature; useful in combination |
| Click pacing to mimic humans | 2, 3 | Aggregate temporal patterns across devices, not single-click timing |
| Distributed low-volume per device | 3 | Population-level features (device clusters, app-publisher patterns) |
| Targeting low-CPM ad networks | All | Cross-network signal sharing (out of scope for this project) |

### 4.3 Design Implications

The detection design must:

- **Not depend on any single feature.** Adversaries can falsify any individual signal. Robustness comes from feature combinations.
- **Use aggregate features heavily.** Per-IP, per-device, per-app rolling aggregates are harder to falsify than per-click attributes.
- **Be retrained regularly.** Adversaries adapt; static models decay. Retraining cadence is part of the system spec, not an afterthought.
- **Treat published policy as adversary intelligence.** Anything we publish about how we detect, sophisticated adversaries will read. Some detection logic is deliberately not publishable; this project documents the *methodology* without leaking specific decision boundaries.

## 5. Policy Specification (Summary)

Full policy in `policy.md`. Summary here:

### 5.1 What Constitutes Click Fraud (For This Project)

A click is considered fraudulent if any of the following apply:

**P1.** It originates from automated software intended to generate clicks (bots, emulator farms).

**P2.** It is performed by a human paid specifically to click ads without genuine interest (paid click farms).

**P3.** It is generated by a compromised device whose owner did not consent to the click activity.

**P4.** It is generated to deplete a competitor's ad budget (competitor sabotage).

In the TalkingData dataset, ground truth is approximated by the binary label `is_attributed` (did the click result in an app install). Clicks that did not lead to installs are *not all fraudulent* — most non-converting clicks are simply uninterested real users. The label is a useful but imperfect proxy. The policy document discusses this measurement gap in detail.

### 5.2 Severity Tiers

| Tier | Definition | Default action |
|---|---|---|
| Severity 1 | High-confidence fraud (model score ≥ T_block) | Auto-block click; do not charge advertiser |
| Severity 2 | Suspected fraud (T_review ≤ score < T_block) | Route to human review queue |
| Severity 3 | Borderline (score < T_review but flagged by sampling) | Spot-check sample for QA and drift monitoring |
| Clean | Score below all thresholds and not sampled | No action |

T_block and T_review are configuration values, set via the cost-based methodology in `tradeoffs.md`.

### 5.3 Gray Areas Documented

The full policy lists 12 gray-area scenarios (e.g., "user accidentally clicks then immediately backs out — fraud?"); these are deliberately surfaced rather than hidden.

## 6. System Design

Full architecture in `architecture.md`. Summary diagram and key decisions here.

### 6.1 High-Level Architecture

```
[Raw click data]
       │
       ▼
[Data validation & schema checks]          ← src/data/
       │
       ▼
[Feature engineering (SQL + Python)]       ← src/features/, sql/
       │   ├─ Per-click features
       │   ├─ Rolling aggregates
       │   └─ Graph / co-occurrence features
       ▼
[Model inference]                          ← src/models/
       │
       ▼
[Score calibration]                        ← src/models/calibration.py
       │
       ▼
[Triage routing]                           ← src/triage/
       │
       ├──► [Auto-block]
       ├──► [Human review queue (HTML report)]
       └──► [QA sample]
       │
       ▼
[Audit log + metrics emission]             ← src/sentry/audit/
```

### 6.2 Key Design Decisions

| Decision | Choice | Alternative considered | Why this choice |
|---|---|---|---|
| Modeling framework | LightGBM | XGBoost, CatBoost, sklearn | Fastest training, native categorical handling, robust on tabular fraud |
| Feature store | SQL (DuckDB) | pandas-only | DuckDB lets feature work mirror real T&S SQL workflows |
| Train/test split | Time-based (by day) | Random | Random splits leak future information; time-based mimics production |
| Calibration | Isotonic regression | Platt scaling | Isotonic is more flexible; we have data volume to support it |
| Sampling for class imbalance | None (rely on class weights) | SMOTE, undersampling | Synthetic samples on this data type are known to harm calibration |
| Evaluation primary metric | PR-AUC | ROC-AUC, F1 | PR-AUC is appropriate for rare positive class; ROC-AUC misleads |
| Threshold selection | Cost-based | Maximize F1 | Real systems are tuned to cost, not metric maxima |

### 6.3 Data Flow Constraints

- The dataset is ~7 GB on disk. Working in DuckDB allows out-of-core SQL operations without loading all data into RAM.
- A documented sampled subset is used during development for iteration speed; final evaluation runs on the full test partition.

## 7. Detection Methodology

### 7.1 Feature Strategy

Features are organized into four families. Each family is documented because *why* a feature exists matters more than that it exists.

**F1. Per-click attributes (low-signal individually, useful in combination)**

- App, channel, device type, OS — categorical features capturing inventory characteristics
- Hour of day, day of week — temporal context
- IP-app, IP-device, IP-os co-occurrence counts within the click

**F2. Velocity and burst features (target Tier 1 adversaries)**

- Clicks per IP per hour
- Clicks per IP-app pair per hour
- Click inter-arrival time statistics per IP
- Standard deviation of inter-arrival time (real humans are bursty; bots are often too regular)

**F3. Aggregate behavioral features (target Tier 2)**

- Conversion rate of IP, app, device over the prior 24 hours
- Conversion rate of IP-app pair (often the strongest single feature for this dataset)
- Number of distinct apps clicked per IP per day
- Number of distinct devices per IP per day

**F4. Graph-style features (target Tier 3)**

- IP-app bipartite graph: degree of the IP node, degree of the app node
- App publisher reputation aggregates (mean conversion rate of all clicks on the app over baseline window)
- Network ASN-level aggregates (using lookups; if ASN data is unavailable in the dataset, this family is reduced)

> **Shipped (Task 3.3):** F4 was descoped to the cheap degree-style counts only — these were absorbed into F3 as the distinct-entity features (`f3_ip_distinct_apps_24hr`, `f3_ip_distinct_devices_24hr`, `f3_ip_distinct_oses_24hr`, `f3_app_distinct_ips_24hr`). The full bipartite-graph / cluster-similarity work (the real Tier-3 counter) did not earn its complexity within the time budget and is documented as future work in `architecture.md` and `adversary-model.md`. The consequence is stated honestly in the adversary doc: Sentry covers Tiers 1–2 well and the easy edge of Tier 3, but not coordinated low-volume Tier-3 fraud. See `decisions.md` (Task 3.3).

### 7.2 Train/Test Split

Time-based: earlier days for training, the latest day for testing. A validation window is held out for hyperparameter tuning, separate from the final test day. This mirrors how a production model would be retrained.

### 7.3 Model

LightGBM binary classifier with class weights inversely proportional to class frequency. Hyperparameters tuned on the validation window via Bayesian optimization (Optuna), with a fixed compute budget documented in `decisions.md`.

### 7.4 Calibration

Raw LightGBM scores are not probabilities. We apply isotonic regression fit on the validation set so that the score `0.7` means "we estimate ~70% probability of fraud." Calibrated probabilities are required for cost-based thresholding (§8).

### 7.5 Explainability

For each flagged case routed to a human reviewer, the system emits the top-5 contributing features via SHAP values. This is required for the reviewer persona (§3.1) and is also a real T&S production requirement.

## 8. Enforcement Workflow

### 8.1 Three-Tier Triage

Detailed thresholds in `tradeoffs.md`. Logic:

```
if score >= T_block:
    action = AUTO_BLOCK
    log_to_audit(case, action, model_version, top_features)
elif score >= T_review:
    action = HUMAN_REVIEW
    enqueue(case, priority=score)
elif random() < QA_SAMPLE_RATE:
    action = QA_SAMPLE
    enqueue(case, priority=QA_PRIORITY)
else:
    action = ALLOW
```

### 8.2 Threshold Selection (Cost-Based)

Let:

- `C_FP` = cost of a false positive (blocking a real click). Approximated as the expected revenue impact of one blocked legitimate click.
- `C_FN` = cost of a false negative (allowing a fraudulent click). Approximated as the average advertiser charge per click.
- `C_review` = cost of one human review. Approximated as reviewer time × loaded hourly cost.

For each candidate threshold `T`, the expected cost is:

```
E[cost(T)] = (FP_rate(T) × N × C_FP)
           + (FN_rate(T) × N × C_FN)
           + (Review_volume(T) × C_review)
```

We select the `T_block` and `T_review` pair that minimizes total expected cost subject to the constraint that the review queue volume does not exceed reviewer capacity (SM6).

The specific dollar values used are documented and clearly marked as illustrative; the methodology, not the numbers, is the deliverable.

### 8.3 Audit Log

Every action (auto-block, queued for review, allowed) is logged with:

- Case ID, timestamp
- Model version and policy version
- Score, threshold values active at the time
- Top contributing features
- Final disposition (after human review, if applicable)

This log is what makes the system *auditable*, which is the property real T&S systems are graded on.

### 8.4 Appeals Process [OUT OF SCOPE — discussed]

A real system requires an appeals workflow: advertisers and publishers can contest decisions. The data model supports this (audit log is the substrate), but no UI or workflow is implemented in this project. The architecture document discusses what a production appeals system would look like.

## 9. Evaluation Framework

### 9.1 Metrics Computed

For every model version, the pipeline computes and stores:

- PR-AUC (primary), ROC-AUC (secondary)
- Precision and recall at multiple operating points
- Calibration plots and Brier score
- Cost curve as a function of threshold
- Per-feature SHAP importance
- Confusion matrix at the selected threshold

### 9.2 Ablation Study

To demonstrate that each feature family contributes meaningfully, we run an ablation: train the model with one family removed at a time and report the PR-AUC delta. Expected outcome: F3 (aggregate behavioral) is the highest-impact family; F4 (graph) is the most expensive but smallest gain. The ablation is itself a portfolio artifact — it shows analytical rigor.

### 9.3 Comparison to Baseline

The trained model is compared against three baselines:

- **B1.** Random scoring (sanity check)
- **B2.** Conversion-rate-only model (one feature: historical conversion rate of the IP-app pair)
- **B3.** Logistic regression on F1+F2 features only (linear, simple)

A senior reader expects baselines. Their absence is a tell of junior work.

## 10. Monitoring, Drift, and Feedback Loops

### 10.1 Drift Concerns

Three forms of drift are anticipated:

- **Population drift.** The distribution of legitimate user behavior shifts (new apps gain popularity, time-of-day patterns change). Detected by monitoring feature distributions.
- **Concept drift.** Adversary adaptation changes the relationship between features and fraud. Detected by monitoring precision/recall on the human-reviewed sample.
- **Label drift.** The definition of "fraud" itself changes via policy updates. Tracked by versioning the policy.

### 10.2 Monitoring Plan

The repository emits a daily monitoring report containing:

- Feature distribution drift scores (Population Stability Index) vs. training distribution
- Confusion-matrix updates from completed human reviews
- Retraining trigger: PSI > 0.25 on any top-10 feature, *or* monthly cadence, whichever comes first

### 10.3 Feedback Loop Hazards [OPEN QUESTION]

A naively designed system creates a feedback loop: cases auto-blocked never get human-reviewed, so the model never learns from its own decisions. The design mitigates this with QA sampling (Severity 3) on auto-blocked cases. The sampling rate is an open question — too high wastes reviewer time, too low fails to detect drift in the auto-block region. The PRD documents this as unresolved rather than papering over it.

## 11. Risks and Failure Modes

| Risk | Likelihood | Impact | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Time-based split leaks information via shared IPs | Medium | High (inflates metrics) | Document split boundaries; spot-check feature leakage |
| Class imbalance leads to degenerate model | Low | High | Class weights, PR-AUC monitoring, sanity-check baselines |
| Adversary reads writeup and adapts | High | Medium | Publish methodology, not specific decision boundaries |
| Reviewer capacity assumed but unvalidated | High | Medium | Document the assumption; surface SM6 prominently |
| Cost assumptions are arbitrary | High | Low (it's a portfolio project) | Mark numbers as illustrative; emphasize the methodology |
| Dataset characteristics don't generalize to other ad networks | Medium | Low | Be explicit about scope: this models *this* dataset |
| Reproducibility breaks across environments | Low | High | Docker, pinned versions, deterministic seeds |
| Project scope creep eats time | High | High | This PRD's non-goals are the kill switch |

## 12. Rollout Plan [Conceptual — Project Is Not Deployed]

In a production setting, the rollout would proceed:

1. **Shadow mode.** Model runs alongside existing systems, predictions logged but no enforcement. Compare to existing system's actions.
2. **Limited auto-block.** Enable auto-block for top-1% confidence cases only. Heavily QA-sampled.
3. **Full triage.** All three tiers active, full review queue staffed.
4. **Continuous improvement.** Retraining cadence established, drift monitoring live.

A real PRD would include rollback criteria at each phase. For this project, we describe the methodology without simulating it.

## 13. Open Questions

These are deliberately surfaced rather than hidden. A senior PRD has visible unresolved questions; a junior one pretends everything is settled.

**Q1.** What is the appropriate QA sampling rate on auto-blocked cases? Project will explore three rates (1%, 5%, 10%) and document the tradeoff, but a defensible answer requires production data not available here.

**Q2.** Should the model be a single global model or per-segment (e.g., per-country, per-app-category)? Single global model is the chosen scope; per-segment is documented as future work.

**Q3.** How should the reviewer's decisions feed back into model training without amplifying labeling bias? The audit log captures the data needed; the methodology for using it safely is non-trivial and is documented as future work.

**Q4.** What is the right cadence for retraining? Heuristics in §10.2 are used; a principled answer requires running the system for months.

**Q5.** How would adversarial robustness be evaluated? Outside this project's scope; flagged for the GenAI red-teaming project that may follow.

### Appendix B — Glossary

- **PR-AUC.** Precision-Recall Area Under Curve. Appropriate for imbalanced classification because it does not credit the model for correctly identifying the abundant negative class.
- **ROC-AUC.** Receiver Operating Characteristic AUC. Standard but misleading on rare-positive tasks because it weights TNR equally with TPR.
- **PSI.** Population Stability Index. Compares feature distributions across time windows; > 0.25 conventionally indicates significant drift.
- **SHAP.** SHapley Additive exPlanations. Method for attributing model predictions to input features.

## 17. Changelog — plan vs. reality

This PRD was written before implementation. The sections above are kept close
to their original form on purpose — "what I planned" is itself interview
material. This changelog records where the build diverged from the plan and
why. Full reasoning for each item is in `docs/decisions.md`.

**Updated 2026-06-17 (Week 7, Task 7.4):**

- **PR-AUC target (G1 / SM1).** Original target "PR-AUC ≥ 0.85" was a
  metric-scale error — the cited 0.86–0.98 Kaggle figures are ROC-AUC, the
  metric that competition was scored on. Corrected to a dual-metric goal: test
  ROC-AUC 0.972 (≥ 0.95 bar met) and test PR-AUC 0.559 reported honestly
  (≈220× the base rate, far above baselines). Amended inline at G1 on
  2026-06-10; see `decisions.md`.

- **Human review tier is uneconomic at the illustrative costs (SM5 / SM6 /
  §8).** The cost sweep drove the review band to zero width: reviewing a click
  ($0.50) costs more than the worst auto-decision error it prevents ($0.30).
  So the cost-optimal policy is a two-way split at fraud score 0.5 — auto-action
  rate ~100% (vs the 60–80% SM5 band, which assumed a populated review tier)
  and review load 0% (vs SM6's ≤ 0.5%). The three-tier design and router are
  fully built; the finding is *about* the tier, not a reason to remove it. See
  `tradeoffs.md` and `decisions.md` (Tasks 5.2/5.3).

- **Enforcement demo uses quantile thresholds, not cost-optimal ones.** Because
  the `is_attributed` proxy makes ~99.8% of traffic "fraud-suspect," a fixed
  cost-optimal cutoff blocks nearly everything and the review queue is empty.
  The `sentry enforce` demo therefore routes on raw fraud-score quantiles to
  populate all three tiers for the review-queue artifact. This is artifact
  population, not policy selection (§3.6). Stated plainly in `policy.md` §4.

- **F4 graph family descoped (§7.1).** Reduced to degree-style distinct-entity
  counts (absorbed into F3); the full bipartite-graph / cluster work is future
  work. Documented at §7.1 above and in the adversary model as a known
  Tier-3 coverage gap.

- **Hyperparameter tuning: a kept negative result.** Optuna on a subsample
  produced params that scored *worse* on full data (val PR-AUC 0.476 vs the
  default's 0.562) because hyperparameters don't transfer across data size. The
  untuned default `lgbm-v0.1.0` is the shipped model; the negative result is
  documented rather than hidden (Task 4.4). SM-wise this does not change any
  target; it changes which model is canonical.

- **Two high-cardinality interaction features excluded from the model.**
  `f1_ip_app_interaction` / `f1_ip_device_interaction` remain in the feature
  store but are not model inputs — they are a memorization trap and OOM the
  container at scale. The model trains on 26 numeric features (Task 4.3).

- **Feature store is a 10% time-stratified sample.** `v0.5.0` holds ~18.5M rows
  (train 11.1M / val 3.7M / test 3.6M) sampled from the full 184.9M-row stream,
  with full-history windows and all-time aggregates. This is the documented
  development/eval scale within the 3.8 GB container; the methodology is
  full-scale, the row count is sampled.

- **`config.py` (pydantic-settings) not built.** The planned config module
  (§ project structure) proved unnecessary — configuration lives in CLI flags
  plus the `DATA_DIR` Docker mount. `config.py` is an intentional empty stub
  (YAGNI). Documented in `architecture.md` §4.

- **`src/monitoring/` never existed.** The §6.1 diagram originally pointed audit
  logging at `src/monitoring/`; the implemented home is `src/sentry/audit/`
  (schema + DuckDB writer). Diagram corrected.

- **The `is_attributed`-as-fraud proxy is loose, and the docs now say so
  everywhere.** Non-conversion ≠ fraud; most non-converting clicks are
  uninterested real users. The limitation is foregrounded in `policy.md` §2,
  `adversary-model.md` §5, and `data-dictionary.md`. A production system needs a
  real fraud label.
